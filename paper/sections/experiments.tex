\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}
%% ===================================================================

We evaluate AEGIS on 9 datasets across 4 domains with 10 random seeds each (seeds: 42, 137, 271, 314, 1729, 2718, 3141, 5772, 6561, 9999). All experiments are implemented in PyTorch~\cite{paszke2019pytorch}. Graph benchmark experiments use IGNN~\cite{gu2020implicit} with hidden dimension $d = 64$, spectral-norm constrained weights~\cite{miyato2018spectral}, and early stopping on validation accuracy over 200 epochs. Power flow experiments use ContractiveGCN-PF, an IGNN variant for AC power flow (\cref{sec:power_flow}).

\textbf{Datasets.} We use five graph benchmarks: Cora (2,708 nodes), Citeseer (3,327), and Pubmed (19,717) from the Planetoid collection~\cite{sen2008collective}; Amazon Photo (7,650) from the Amazon co-purchase graphs~\cite{shchur2018pitfalls}; and WikiCS (11,701) from Wikipedia-based benchmarks~\cite{mernyei2020wiki}. For power flow, we use four IEEE standard test cases~\cite{ieee2024testcases}: case14 (14 buses), case30 (30), case57 (57), and case118 (118), with training data generated via PandaPower~\cite{thurner2018pandapower}.

\textbf{Notation.} All formal bounds (\cref{thm:phase_transition}) use the operator-norm contraction constant $\kappa = \norm{J_z}_2$. Tables report the spectral radius $\rho(J_z)$ as a diagnostic quantity. Since non-normality is mild ($\eta = 1.02$--$1.28$, \cref{sec:convergence}), we have $\kappa \approx \rho$ in practice; the $\ecrit$ values computed with $\rho$ are at most 28\% optimistic relative to the $\kappa$-based formal threshold.

\subsection{Certificate Comparison: Deterministic vs.\ Smoothing}
\label{sec:certificates}

We compare AEGIS's deterministic first-order radii (\cref{prop:radius}) against randomized smoothing~\cite{bojchevski2020efficient} with Gaussian noise on existing edges ($\sigma \in \{0.01, 0.05, 0.10, 0.15\}$, 1000 Monte Carlo samples, Clopper-Pearson confidence $1-\alpha = 0.999$). \cref{tab:certificates} reports median certified radius on Cora, Citeseer, and WikiCS (10 seeds, 50-node subgraphs).

Citeseer & $\ms{.045}{.012}$ & .025 & .123 & $\ms{.233}{.013}$ & $\ms{.091}{.069}$ \\
WikiCS   & $\ms{.040}{.004}$ & .025 & .123 & $\ms{.246}{.000}$ & $\ms{.341}{.017}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The comparison reveals a qualitative difference rather than a simple radius ordering. At $\sigma \leq 0.10$, smoothing radii are \emph{constant} across all nodes and datasets: every node receives the identical maximum-possible radius $r = \sigma \cdot \Phi^{-1}(p_{\mathrm{lower}})$ because all 1000 Monte Carlo samples predict correctly. This produces larger absolute radii than AEGIS (0.123 vs.\ 0.046 at $\sigma = 0.05$) but provides zero per-node differentiation. The certificates do not distinguish between structurally vulnerable and robust nodes. At $\sigma = 0.15$, smoothing begins to differentiate (some nodes lose prediction consistency), but with high variance.

AEGIS radii, by contrast, are structurally informative: median radius 0.041--0.046 with per-node variation that reflects actual equilibrium sensitivity. Nodes in dense, well-connected regions receive larger radii ($r_v \approx 0.09$), while boundary nodes in sensitive positions get smaller ones ($r_v \approx 0.01$). This per-node differentiation is the key advantage for applications that need vulnerability ranking rather than uniform worst-case bounds. The two approaches are complementary: smoothing provides uniform probabilistic guarantees, while AEGIS provides tight, deterministic, structurally differentiated first-order radii.

\subsection{Phase Transition and Scalability}
\label{sec:phase_transition}
\label{sec:scalability}

\textbf{Phase transition.} To validate \cref{thm:phase_transition}, we scale the adjacency to target $\rho \in \{0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.85, 0.9, 0.95, 0.99\}$ and measure the fixed-point shift at $\varepsilon = 0.01$. The shift amplifies by $83\times$ as $\rho$ increases from 0.3 to 0.99, confirming the $1/(1-\kappa)$ amplification factor.

\textbf{Scalability.} On a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU, the full AEGIS pipeline takes 0.5s ($N=20$), 1.3s ($N=50$), 2.8s ($N=100$), and 8.1s ($N=200$), dominated by the $O(D \cdot N^2)$ Jacobian $J_A$ computation. The practical limit is $N \approx 200$ ($D = 12{,}800$), covering all IEEE test cases and typical ego-network sizes.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Sensitivity}
\label{sec:hyperparams}

We evaluate sensitivity to the two key architectural hyperparameters: hidden dimension $d$ and spectral-norm constraint $c$ (the upper bound on $\norm{W}_2$).

\textbf{Hidden dimension.} Varying $d \in \{16, 32, 64, 128\}$ on Cora: tightness is 1.012--1.013 for all values, confirming robustness to architecture width. Accuracy improves from 74.7\% ($d=16$) to 79.1\% ($d=64$). The critical budget and median radius are stable across $d$: $\ecrit \in [0.53, 0.56]$, $r_v \in [0.037, 0.051]$.

\textbf{Spectral norm constraint.} Varying $c \in \{0.5, 0.7, 0.9, 0.95\}$ reveals the accuracy-robustness tradeoff predicted by \cref{thm:phase_transition}. At $c = 0.5$: $\rho = 0.25$, $\ecrit = 1.50$, median $r_v = 0.090$, giving the largest radii but accuracy drops to 72.1\%. At $c = 0.9$: $\rho = 0.45$, $\ecrit = 0.61$, $r_v = 0.051$, accuracy peaks at 80.6\%. Radii shrink by $1.8\times$ from $c = 0.5$ to $c = 0.9$ as $1/(1 - \kappa)$ grows, consistent with the theoretical amplification factor in \cref{eq:shift_bound}.

\subsection{DEQ Convergence Diagnostics}
\label{sec:convergence}

We report convergence statistics for the IGNN fixed-point iteration across all 9 datasets (10 seeds, relative tolerance $\norm{Z_{k+1} - Z_k}_F < 10^{-5} \cdot \max(\norm{Z_k}_F, 1)$). Convergence is 100\% across all datasets and seeds. Graph benchmarks require $28 \pm 7$ (Amazon) to $113 \pm 39$ (Cora) iterations, while IEEE grids converge faster: $11 \pm 2$ (case14) to $44 \pm 13$ (case57). The pseudospectral index is $\eta = 1.02$--$1.05$ on graph benchmarks and $\eta = 1.20$--$1.28$ on IEEE grids, confirming mild non-normality across all domains. No seed on any dataset failed to converge, consistent with the enforced contractivity $\kappa = \norm{J_z}_2 < 1$ via spectral normalization~\cite{miyato2018spectral} of $W$.
