\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}
%% ===================================================================

We evaluate AEGIS on 9 datasets across 4 domains with 10 random seeds each (seeds: 42, 137, 271, 314, 1729, 2718, 3141, 5772, 6561, 9999). All experiments are implemented in PyTorch~\cite{paszke2019pytorch}. Graph benchmark experiments use IGNN~\cite{gu2020implicit} with hidden dimension $d = 64$, spectral-norm constrained weights~\cite{miyato2018spectral}, and early stopping on validation accuracy over 200 epochs. Power flow experiments use ContractiveGCN-PF, an IGNN variant for AC power flow (\cref{sec:power_flow}).

\textbf{Datasets.} We use five graph benchmarks: Cora (2,708 nodes), Citeseer (3,327), and Pubmed (19,717) from the Planetoid collection~\cite{sen2008collective}; Amazon Photo (7,650) from the Amazon co-purchase graphs~\cite{shchur2018pitfalls}; and WikiCS (11,701) from Wikipedia-based benchmarks~\cite{mernyei2020wiki}. For power flow, we use four IEEE standard test cases~\cite{ieee2024testcases}: case14 (14 buses), case30 (30), case57 (57), and case118 (118), with training data generated via PandaPower~\cite{thurner2018pandapower}.

\textbf{Notation.} All formal bounds (\cref{thm:phase_transition}) use the operator-norm contraction constant $\kappa = \norm{J_z}_2$. Tables report the spectral radius $\rho(J_z)$ as a diagnostic quantity. Since non-normality is mild ($\eta = 1.02$--$1.28$, \cref{sec:convergence}), we have $\kappa \approx \rho$ in practice; the $\ecrit$ values computed with $\rho$ are at most 28\% optimistic relative to the $\kappa$-based formal threshold.

\textbf{Surrogate baseline.} We also compared against Mettack~\cite{zugner2019adversarial} (GCN~\cite{kipf2017semi} surrogate): AEGIS inflicts 3--10$\times$ more damage at every budget (30/30 wins across $k=1,\ldots,5$ edge removals). However, this gap largely reflects surrogate-to-IGNN architectural mismatch rather than AEGIS's analytical superiority alone. The more meaningful comparison is the adaptive attacker below, which accesses the same IFT gradients as AEGIS.

\subsection{Radius Comparison: AEGIS vs.\ Smoothing}
\label{sec:certificates}

At $\sigma \leq 0.10$, smoothing radii are \emph{constant} across all nodes: every node receives the identical radius $r = \sigma \cdot \Phi^{-1}(p_{\mathrm{lower}})$ because all Monte Carlo samples predict correctly. This yields larger absolute radii than AEGIS (0.123 vs.\ 0.046 at $\sigma = 0.05$) but zero per-node differentiation. AEGIS radii are structurally informative: dense-region nodes get $r_v \approx 0.09$, boundary nodes get $r_v \approx 0.01$. The two approaches are complementary: smoothing provides uniform probabilistic guarantees, AEGIS provides structurally differentiated first-order radii for vulnerability ranking.

\subsection{Adaptive Attack Evaluation}
\label{sec:adaptive}

To address this, we implement an \emph{adaptive attacker} that differentiates through the IGNN fixed-point iteration via the same IFT gradients AEGIS uses for sensitivity analysis. This attacker optimizes perturbations directly against the IGNN loss surface using projected gradient descent~\cite{madry2018towards} (PGD, 50 steps, step size $\varepsilon/10$).

\subsection{Phase Transition and Scalability}
\label{sec:phase_transition}
\label{sec:scalability}

\textbf{Phase transition.} To validate \cref{thm:phase_transition}, we scale the adjacency to target $\rho \in \{0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.85, 0.9, 0.95, 0.99\}$ and measure the fixed-point shift at $\varepsilon = 0.01$. The shift amplifies by $83\times$ as $\rho$ increases from 0.3 to 0.99, confirming the $1/(1-\kappa)$ amplification factor.

\textbf{Scalability.} On a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU, the full AEGIS pipeline takes 0.5s ($N=20$), 1.3s ($N=50$), 2.8s ($N=100$), and 8.1s ($N=200$), dominated by the $O(D \cdot N^2)$ Jacobian $J_A$ computation. The practical limit is $N \approx 200$ ($D = 12{,}800$), covering all IEEE test cases and typical ego-network sizes.

\subsection{Sensitivity and Convergence}
\label{sec:hyperparams}
\label{sec:convergence}

\textbf{Hyperparameters.} Varying hidden dimension $d \in \{16, 32, 64, 128\}$ on Cora: tightness is stable at 1.012--1.013 and $\ecrit \in [0.53, 0.56]$. Varying spectral-norm constraint $c \in \{0.5, 0.7, 0.9, 0.95\}$ reveals the accuracy-robustness tradeoff predicted by \cref{thm:phase_transition}: at $c = 0.5$, median $r_v = 0.090$ but accuracy is 72.1\%; at $c = 0.9$, $r_v = 0.051$ with accuracy 80.6\%. Radii shrink $1.8\times$ as $1/(1-\kappa)$ grows.

\textbf{Convergence.} 100\% convergence across all 9 datasets and 10 seeds (relative tol.\ $10^{-5}$). Iterations range from $28 \pm 7$ (Amazon) to $113 \pm 39$ (Cora); IEEE grids converge faster ($11$--$44$). Pseudospectral index $\eta = 1.02$--$1.28$ across all domains, confirming mild non-normality and validating the $\kappa$-based bounds.

\subsection{Extension to Explicit GNNs}
\label{sec:explicit_extension}

A key question is whether the $S_c$ framework extends beyond implicit models. For a $K$-layer explicit GNN, there is no equilibrium, but the hidden representation $Z_K$ is still a differentiable function of $A$. We define the \emph{unrolled sensitivity matrix} $S_K = \partial \mathrm{vec}(Z_K) / \partial \mathrm{vec}(A)$ (computed via finite differences on the forward pass), construct $S_c$ from $S_K$ identically to the IGNN case, and apply the same SVD attack and vulnerability ranking.

\cref{tab:explicit} applies the $S_c$ framework to six explicit GNN architectures on Cora. The analysis succeeds for five of six: GCN~\cite{kipf2017semi}, GIN, GraphSAGE, and APPNP all achieve tightness between 1.00 and 1.02, with attack advantage ranging from 2.8$\times$ (SAGE) to 9.1$\times$ (GCN-4). GraphSAGE and GCN-4 produce the strongest vulnerability rankings ($\tau = +0.49$ and $+0.48$), surpassing IGNN ($+0.32$). APPNP, whose weight-tied PageRank propagation is closest to IGNN's iterative structure, achieves $\tau = +0.36$ with 94\% accuracy.

GAT is the exception: its softmax attention masking creates near-discontinuous gradients with respect to $A$, causing the finite-difference Jacobian to produce unreliable sensitivity estimates ($\tau = -0.26$). Extending the analysis to attention-based architectures would require differentiating through the attention computation analytically rather than via finite differences.

The key takeaway is that the $S_c$ framework is not inherently tied to implicit models. The theoretical guarantees (\cref{thm:phase_transition}: bounded radii, critical budget) remain specific to contractive implicit GNNs, but the practical vulnerability analysis (tightness, attack directions, vulnerability ranking) transfers to any GNN whose message passing is smoothly differentiable with respect to the adjacency matrix.
