\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}
%% ===================================================================

We evaluate \AEGIS on 9 datasets across 4 domains with 10 random seeds each (seeds: 42, 137, 271, 314, 1729, 2718, 3141, 5772, 6561, 9999). All experiments use PyTorch~\cite{paszke2019pytorch}.

\textbf{Implementation.} IGNN~\cite{gu2020implicit}: input projection $U \in \R^{d_{\text{in}} \times 64}$, spectral-normalized $W \in \R^{64 \times 64}$~\cite{miyato2018spectral}, readout head $\R^{64 \times C}$; operator $F(Z) = \text{ReLU}(\Ahat Z W^\top + U(X))$; fixed-point iteration (max 50 steps, tolerance $10^{-5}$); Adam optimizer (lr $0.01$, weight decay $5 \times 10^{-4}$), early stopping on validation accuracy over 200 epochs. ContractiveGCN-PF: same architecture with 5 bus features and 2-head readout ($\Delta|V|$, $\Delta\theta$). Subgraph extraction: BFS from highest-degree node, capped at 50 nodes to guarantee connectivity. Randomized smoothing baseline: Gaussian $\sigma = 0.01$, 200 MC samples, Clopper-Pearson $\alpha = 0.001$. Mettack baseline: Meta-Self GCN surrogate on IGNN pseudo-labels, sign-corrected gradient for edge scoring.

\textbf{Datasets.} We use five graph benchmarks: Cora (2,708 nodes), Citeseer (3,327), and Pubmed (19,717) from the Planetoid collection~\cite{sen2008collective}; Amazon Photo (7,650) from the Amazon co-purchase graphs~\cite{shchur2018pitfalls}; and WikiCS (11,701) from Wikipedia-based benchmarks~\cite{mernyei2020wiki}. For power flow, we use four IEEE standard test cases~\cite{ieee2024testcases}: case14 (14 buses), case30 (30), case57 (57), and case118 (118), with training data generated via PandaPower~\cite{thurner2018pandapower}.

\textbf{Notation.} All formal bounds (\cref{thm:phase_transition}) and tables use the operator-norm contraction constant $\kappa = \norm{J_z}_2$, estimated via power iteration. Since non-normality is mild across our datasets ($\eta = 1.02$--$1.28$, \cref{sec:convergence}), the $\kappa$-based bounds are at most 28\% more conservative than spectral-radius bounds; we report only $\kappa$ because the formal guarantees (\cref{thm:phase_transition}) require operator-norm contractivity, and $\eta$ near unity confirms that $\rho(J_z)$ would yield similar values. The matrix-free pipeline (\cref{sec:framework}) sets the Neumann-series depth adaptively from~$\kappa$.

\textbf{Surrogate baseline.} We also compared against Mettack~\cite{zugner2019adversarial} (GCN~\cite{kipf2017semi} surrogate): \AEGIS inflicts 3--10$\times$ more damage at every budget (149/150 wins across $k=1,\ldots,5$ edge removals, 3 datasets, 10 seeds). However, this gap largely reflects surrogate-to-IGNN architectural mismatch rather than \AEGIS's analytical superiority alone. The more meaningful comparison is the adaptive attacker below, which accesses the same IFT gradients as \AEGIS.

\subsection{Radius Comparison: \AEGIS vs.\ Smoothing}
\label{sec:certificates}

We compare \AEGIS sensitivity radii against randomized smoothing~\cite{bojchevski2020efficient} ($\sigma \in \{0.01, 0.05, 0.10, 0.15\}$, 1000 MC samples, 10 seeds). At $\sigma \leq 0.10$, smoothing radii are \emph{constant} across all nodes ($r = \sigma \cdot \Phi^{-1}(p_\mathrm{lower})$) because all samples predict correctly, yielding larger absolute radii (0.123 vs.\ \AEGIS's 0.046 at $\sigma = 0.05$) but zero per-node differentiation. \AEGIS radii are structurally informative: dense-region nodes get $r_v \approx 0.09$, boundary nodes $r_v \approx 0.01$. The two are complementary: smoothing provides uniform probabilistic guarantees, \AEGIS provides structurally differentiated first-order sensitivity radii for vulnerability ranking.

\subsection{Phase Transition and Scalability}
\label{sec:phase_transition}
\label{sec:scalability}

\textbf{Phase transition.} Scaling $\kappa$ from 0.3 to 0.99 amplifies the fixed-point shift by $83\times$ at $\varepsilon = 0.01$, confirming the $1/(1-\kappa)$ divergence predicted by \cref{thm:phase_transition}.

\textbf{Scalability.} The matrix-free pipeline removes the $O((Nd)^3)$ bottleneck. On a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 (24\,GB), the dense path handles $N \leq 200$ (5.4s, 8.9\,GB) but exceeds 24\,GB at $N{=}500$. The matrix-free path scales to the full Cora graph ($N{=}2{,}708$, $Nd{=}173{,}312$) in 78\,s using 1\,GB, with $\sigma_1$ accuracy within 0.03\% of the dense reference at $N{=}200$.

\subsection{Subgraph Size Ablation}
\label{sec:subgraph_ablation}

Varying $N \in \{30, 50, 100, 200\}$ on Cora (10 seeds): tightness is stable at 1.01--1.02 for $N \leq 100$ and degrades to $1.031$ at $N = 200$ (higher $\kappa$). Coverage is 82--89\% across all sizes. We use $N=50$ (tightness $1.013 \pm 0.003$, 1.3s) as the default for subgraph-level experiments: $66\times$ faster than $N=200$ with negligible quality loss. For full-graph analysis, the matrix-free pipeline (\cref{tab:scalability}) enables vulnerability assessment of the entire graph without subgraph extraction. To validate that subgraph extraction preserves vulnerability rankings, we compare per-edge $S_c$ rankings from BFS subgraphs against full-graph rankings on IEEE case14 and case30 (10 seeds). On case14, Kendall $\tau$ between subgraph and full-graph rankings is $0.80 \pm 0.14$ at $N_{\text{sub}}{=}8$ (57\% of nodes) and $0.86 \pm 0.06$ at $N_{\text{sub}}{=}12$ (86\%); P@5 $\geq 0.90$ at all sizes. On case30, $\tau = 0.78 \pm 0.07$ at $N_{\text{sub}}{=}10$ (33\% of nodes) with P@5 $= 0.96 \pm 0.08$, remaining stable through $N_{\text{sub}}{=}25$. The locality assumption underlying subgraph extraction thus holds well for power grids, but may weaken for graphs with strong long-range dependencies.

\subsection{Sensitivity and Convergence}
\label{sec:hyperparams}
\label{sec:convergence}

\textbf{Hyperparameters.} Tightness is stable across $d \in \{16, 32, 64, 128\}$ (1.012--1.013). Varying $c \in \{0.5, 0.7, 0.9, 0.95\}$ reveals the predicted accuracy-robustness tradeoff: $c = 0.5$ gives $r_v = 0.090$ (72.1\% acc); $c = 0.9$ gives $r_v = 0.051$ (80.6\% acc).

\textbf{Convergence.} 100\% convergence across all 9 datasets and 10 seeds. Pseudospectral index $\eta = 1.02$--$1.28$, confirming mild non-normality and validating the $\kappa$-based bounds.

\subsection{Defense-Informed Edge Protection}
\label{sec:defense_ablation}

We evaluate vulnerability-aware protection: mask the top-$k$ edges by $v_{ij}$ from the perturbation space, then re-run the SVD attack on the reduced $S_c$. On Cora (IGNN, $N=50$, 10 seeds): masking the top-5 edges reduces SVD attack damage by $42 \pm 8$\% vs.\ $11 \pm 6$\% for random 5-edge masking. At top-10: $61 \pm 7$\% vs.\ $18 \pm 9$\%. \AEGIS identifies edges whose removal disproportionately reduces vulnerability.

\subsection{Extension to Explicit GNNs}
\label{sec:explicit_extension}

A key question is whether the $S_c$ framework extends beyond implicit models. For a $K$-layer explicit GNN, there is no equilibrium, but the hidden representation $Z_K$ is still a differentiable function of $A$. We define the \emph{unrolled sensitivity matrix} $S_K = \partial \mathrm{vec}(Z_K) / \partial \mathrm{vec}(A)$ (computed via finite differences on the forward pass), construct $S_c$ from $S_K$ identically to the IGNN case, and apply the same SVD attack and vulnerability ranking.

\cref{tab:explicit} applies the $S_c$ framework to six explicit GNN architectures on Cora. All six achieve near-perfect first-order tightness (0.99--1.02), confirming that constrained sensitivity analysis generalizes beyond implicit models. Attack advantage ranges from 2.0$\times$ (SAGE) to 7.6$\times$ (IGNN), showing that $S_c$ identifies the most vulnerable perturbation directions across all architectures. Test accuracy ranges from 76.6\% (GIN) to 82.2\% (APPNP), competitive with deep residual architectures such as GCNII~\cite{chen2020simple}. GAT$^\dagger$ and GCN-4 produce the strongest vulnerability rankings ($\tau = +0.54$ and $+0.49$). APPNP achieves the best accuracy-vulnerability balance ($\tau = +0.35$, 82.2\% accuracy).

GAT requires an edge-weighted formulation where $\Ahat$ values modulate attention weights continuously; standard GAT uses $A$ as a binary mask (zeroing non-edges), so $\partial Z / \partial A_{ij} = 0$ for all existing edges. GATv2~\cite{brody2022how} partially addresses this via dynamic attention, but still applies a binary mask at the neighborhood level. Our edge-weighted variant multiplies attention coefficients by $\Ahat_{ij}$, restoring continuous differentiability. With this modification, GAT achieves tightness 0.99, attack advantage 4.4$\times$, and $\tau = +0.36$.

\textbf{Continuous-to-discrete transfer.} The $\tau$ column in \cref{tab:explicit} directly measures whether continuous $S_c$ vulnerability scores predict discrete edge-removal impact. The brute-force ground truth removes each edge entirely (a discrete, non-infinitesimal perturbation) and re-evaluates the model; $S_c$ scores are derived from continuous first-order sensitivity. Positive $\tau$ across 6 of 7 architectures ($+0.22$ to $+0.54$) demonstrates that continuous first-order analysis is a reliable proxy for discrete structural vulnerability, despite the perturbation-model mismatch. The one exception is GCN-2 ($\tau = -0.04$), likely due to its shallow depth limiting sensitivity differentiation.

\textbf{Comparison with gradient attribution.} Against the same brute-force discrete ground truth, gradient-based edge attribution~\cite{pope2019explainability,ying2019gnnexplainer} produces consistently negative $\tau$ ($-0.07$, $-0.17$, $-0.21$ for IGNN, GCN-2, GIN-2 respectively). Gradient methods optimize for prediction fidelity rather than adversarial vulnerability.

\textbf{Proposition~\ref{prop:explicit} bound tightness.} The ratio (Prop.~4 bound)$/\sigma_1(S_K)$ ranges from 1.4$\times$ (SAGE-2) to 5.9$\times$ (APPNP), with 2-layer models achieving tighter ratios (GCN-2: 1.8$\times$, GIN-2: 2.1$\times$) than deeper ones (GCN-4: 4.2$\times$, GAT-2: 3.7$\times$). The looseness in deeper models reflects direction-dependent cancellations that the norm product overestimates.

\textbf{Degree-weighted $S_c$.} Weighting $S_c$ columns by endpoint degree does not improve ranking: uniform $\tau = +0.042$, degree-weighted $+0.037$, inverse-degree $-0.008$ (GCN-2, 10 seeds). High-degree nodes already contribute larger sensitivity columns via aggregation.

The $S_c$ framework thus generalizes to any GNN whose message passing is smoothly differentiable with respect to edge weights. The theoretical guarantees (\cref{thm:phase_transition}: critical budget, convergence) remain specific to contractive implicit GNNs, but the practical vulnerability analysis (SVD attacks, rankings, first-order sensitivity radii) transfers broadly.
