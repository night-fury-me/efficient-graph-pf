\section{Case Study: Power Flow Contingency}
\label{sec:power_flow}
%% ===================================================================

The experiments above validate \AEGIS on classification benchmarks. We now test whether the vulnerability spectrum captures domain-specific structure by applying it to AC power flow, where the spectrum recovers a classical engineering problem: N-1 contingency analysis. Recent work has applied GNNs to power flow computation~\cite{donon2019graph,bolz2019power} and contingency screening~\cite{nakiganda2023graph}; \AEGIS approaches the same problem from the vulnerability-analysis perspective.

\subsection{Background: N-1 Contingency}

Power grid operators must ensure that no single transmission line failure causes system instability. The standard N-1 contingency assessment removes each line in turn, re-solves the power flow equations, and ranks lines by the severity of the resulting voltage and flow deviations~\cite{wood2014power}. This brute-force procedure scales as $O(|E|)$ full power flow solves, which is expensive for large grids and infeasible for real-time operation.

We observe that \AEGIS's per-edge vulnerability $v_{ij} = \norm{[S_c]_{:,k}}_2$ computes an analogous quantity via a single IFT analysis: edge perturbation maps to line trips, the vulnerability spectrum maps to contingency severity, and critical budget $\ecrit$ maps to a model sensitivity threshold (a mathematical property of the IGNN model, distinct from physical voltage or transient stability margins). The correspondence is approximate (continuous first-order vs.\ discrete removal), analogous to how PTDF/LODF~\cite{wood2014power} linearize the effect of line trips.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

We train ContractiveGCN-PF, an IGNN variant for AC power flow, on five IEEE test cases~\cite{ieee2024testcases} including a 300-bus system to address scalability concerns. Training data is generated via PandaPower's~\cite{thurner2018pandapower} full AC Newton-Raphson solver (2,000 load samples per case, uniformly sampled at 70--130\% of nominal load). The model takes binary adjacency from the admittance matrix and 5 bus features: bus type indicators (slack, PV), active and reactive power injections (P, Q), and voltage magnitude $|V|$. For case14--118 (14--118 buses), full-graph dense analysis is used. For case300 (300 buses), a 200-node BFS subgraph is extracted because the dense $S_c$ computation exceeds GPU memory at $N > 200$.

\textbf{Limitation.} Training data covers uniform load scaling (2,000 samples) but not seasonal or generator-outage variation.

\textbf{Key observations.} First, tightness transfers to the power flow domain ($\approx 1.00$), confirming that the first-order prediction is not domain-specific. Second, ranking correlation ($\tau = 0.37$--$0.72$) is moderate to strong and the operationally relevant P@10 is 0.66--0.87, identifying 7--9 of the top 10 critical lines. Notably, case300 achieves $\tau = +0.72$ and P@10 $= 0.87$ using a 200-node subgraph, demonstrating that \AEGIS scales to grids beyond the 118-bus systems used in prior work.

\textbf{Baseline comparison and timing.} LODF (industry-standard DC screening~\cite{wood2014power}) achieves $\tau = 0.44$--$0.58$; \AEGIS outperforms on larger grids ($\tau = 0.62$--$0.67$ on case57/118) by capturing nonlinear AC effects. LODF computes in $<0.13$\,s (analytic formula from line reactances); \AEGIS requires training (2--10\,s) plus $S_c$ computation (0.2--13\,s), totaling 2--23\,s per case (case300: 23\,s with 200-node subgraph). Brute-force N-1 takes 0.1--2\,s. \AEGIS is thus slower than LODF but provides richer output (vulnerability spectrum, SVD attacks, sensitivity radii) without requiring line-impedance data.

\textbf{N-2 contingency connection.} The leading singular vector $v_1$ of $S_c$ naturally identifies multi-edge vulnerabilities. Extracting the top-5 edges from $|v_1|$ and checking their pairwise impact against brute-force N-2 ground truth (10 seeds, mean per case): edge-level overlap is 40--64\% across cases (edges from the SVD direction appear in the ground-truth critical N-2 pairs), with stronger overlap on larger grids (case118: 64\%). Pair-level overlap is lower (7--18\%) because the SVD direction captures the \emph{most damaging direction} rather than specific pairs, but the constituent edges are individually critical.

\textbf{Rank stability.} Pairwise Kendall $\tau$ of \AEGIS vulnerability rankings across 10 seeds: $+0.40 \pm 0.29$ (case14), $+0.34 \pm 0.24$ (case30), $+0.78 \pm 0.07$ (case57), $+0.67 \pm 0.12$ (case118). Rankings are more stable on larger grids where the topology is richer. On case118, 60\% of the top-10 edges appear in every seed's top-10, and 90\% appear in $\geq$80\% of seeds.

\textbf{Binary vs.\ admittance-weighted adjacency.} Binary adjacency outperforms admittance-weighted (P@10 $= \ms{0.81}{0.10}$ vs.\ $\ms{0.27}{0.13}$ on case118, 10 seeds). Spearman correlation between admittance magnitude and N-1 severity is near zero across all cases ($-0.01$ to $+0.15$), confirming that high-admittance lines are not preferentially critical. This reflects the discrete nature of N-1: a line trip fully removes the line regardless of its impedance, so binary sensitivity (uniform per edge) correctly models the all-or-nothing character. Effective-resistance on the binary graph shows moderate correlation with severity ($\tau = +0.49$ on case14/30), consistent with spectral approaches to network vulnerability~\cite{ronellenfitsch2016global}.

\textbf{Operational caveat.} \AEGIS is a screening layer, not a standalone contingency tool ($\tau = 0.37$--$0.67$ is insufficient for direct operational use).
